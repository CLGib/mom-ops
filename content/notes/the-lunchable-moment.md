Last week I told you delegation was the answer. This week I have to tell you the part nobody warns you about: sometimes it's a disaster first.

Let me tell you about the Lunchable.

## The setup

Last year, I was drowning. And my husband did the most wonderful thing. He said, "Hey, I'll handle the field trip."

Amazing. Love that. Delegation in action.

And he did handle it. He filled out the form. He wrote the check. The trip happened to be near his parents, so he even reached out and invited them along. He emailed the teacher to confirm it was okay.

Everything felt handled. My stress level was zero. It was glorious.

For about a week.

## The unravel

Twenty-four hours before the trip, my mother-in-law starts texting me for the time. Then my husband finds out he has to travel for work. Things are getting a little... wobbly.

Then, the morning of, I get a text from the teacher.

*"Hi Ms. Gibson, quick question. You checked on the field trip form that you would be bringing lunch."*

Lunch.

A lunch I had absolutely not packed. Because I had told my daughter, "just eat the school lunch, you'll be fine."

Except she was not getting the school lunch.

So there I am, texting my mother-in-law like a woman possessed: *"Please, please, please buy her a Lunchable."*

And in that exact moment I had one very clear thought:

**Why did I even delegate this? It would have been easier to just do it myself.**

## That's the downside nobody mentions

When you are the one doing the task, you know every tiny detail. The lunch situation. The time. The backup plan. The backup to the backup.

When someone else does it, there are gaps. And those gaps feel stressful, especially for moms.

Because here is what I have realized. When a mom delegates something, she is not just handing off a task. She is handing off risk. The risk of forgetting something. The risk of disappointing someone. The risk of being judged.

And I don't think dads carry that the same way. You know the joke. A dad takes the kids to the park and it's "what a great dad." A mom does it and it's "oh, there's a mom." A dad brings home pizza and he's a "fun dad." A mom brings home pizza and somehow she's "lazy."

So dads are not constantly scanning for all the little moments where someone might judge them. Moms are. Which means when we delegate, we are not just delegating the task. We are delegating the potential shame that comes with it.

Because when the teacher texted about the forgotten lunch? She texted me. Not my husband. Even though I never touched that form.

You know exactly what I mean.

## But here is the part I didn't expect

Once everything settled down, it was all fine. My daughter ate. My mother-in-law came through with the Lunchable. Nobody was harmed by the absence of a sunbutter sandwich.

And something genuinely valuable happened.

My husband got a glimpse of the mental load. He got to see, for the first time, how many little details live inside a mom's brain at all times. The planning. The backup planning. The backup to the backup planning. The thousand tiny things we are quietly tracking every single day.

The messy delegation did something a smooth one never could have. It made the invisible work visible to someone else.

## So if it went sideways this week, good

Delegation is not efficient at first. It is messy. Things get missed. Sometimes you have to send a frantic Lunchable text.

But over time, something important happens. Other people start to see the work. They start to understand the details. They start to actually share the load.

That is how the mental load gets lighter. Not by doing it all perfectly yourself, but by letting other people learn it, badly at first, until they know it too.

So if you tried handing something off this week and it didn't go perfectly, you are not doing it wrong. You are just at the beginning. Like anything, the first time is rarely pretty. The hundredth time is easy.
